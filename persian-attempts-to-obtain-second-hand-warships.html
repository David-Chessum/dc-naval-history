Persian attempts to obtain second hand warships

In 1922, the principal units of the Persian Navy were two nineteenth century gunboats employed on revenue duties: Persepolis (1885, 1200t) and Mazaffir (1899, 379t), however by 1925 both of these vessels were immobile hulks.

By 1924, the Persian Government had been in negotiation with the German government for several years to procure German warships, and a deal was practically closed for the purchase of three old torpedo boats, but when a special representative of the Prime Minister arrived in Berlin to finish the negotiations, he decided instead to acquire the minesweeper FM24. This boat had previously been sold on 7 April 1922 to an unknown buyer in Hamburg. Originally intended for deployment in the Caspian Sea, the Soviet Government refused permission for the gunboat to transit the Volga, so it was eventually transferred to the Persian Gulf, via the Mediterranean where it was armed in Naples, Italy on account of the arms export restrictions imposed upon Germany by the Treaty of Versailles. Initially named Fatiya, she had been renamed Pahlavi by the time she arrived in Iran on 18 December 1924, and subsequently renamed Shahin in 1935.

On 28 November 1924, General Morteza Khan, the Military Governor of Teheran, sounded the US Charge d’Affaires out as to the possibility of the purchase by the Persian Government of a war vessel from the United States. On 13 January 1925, the State Department advised the Charge d’Affaires that it would not be possible for the United States to negotiate any such sale in view of the provisions of Article 18 of the Washington Naval Treaty.

In late 1928, the Persian Government privately engaged the services of two Italian reserve naval officers, Commander De Zan and Commander Del Prato to take charge of the organisation, operations, and maintenance of a fleet of revenue cutters and other units which the Persian Government hoped to create in the Persian Gulf.

An American consular report in February 1929 reported that the Persian Government had announced the intention to rebuild the navy. It was reported that it was hoped to procure five more vessels, and that dockyards would be developed. It was believed that the object of the navy was to chase smugglers - especially of arms, to enforce quarantine regulations, and to suppress the slave trade. It was reported that the Persian Government hoped that by policing the Persian Gulf itself, it would render less necessary the presence there of British patrols and consequently diminish British influence over the independent sheikhs in the region. The 1929-30 Persian budget included funding for “the purchase and repair of boats and expenses of foreign functionaries for the marine.” This ultimately led to the ordering from Italian yards of two 950-ton sloops (Babr and Palang) and four 331-ton motor patrol boats (Charogh class), later supplemented by six small motor gunboats also built in Italy.

On 20 August 1929, Abdolhossein Teymourtash, Persia’s Minister of Court, in discussion with Sir R. Clive, British Minister in Teheran, made a suggestion that Britain offer the Persian Government free of charge four vessels of 1,000 tons, and six vessels of 200 tons, as one element of the compensation for recognising the independence of Bahrain. This request was re-iterated in writing on 31 March 1930.

At a meeting on 1 April 1930, Clive told Teymourtash that there could be no question of Britain presenting the Persian Government with any ships at all, as this was precluded under the Washington Naval Treaty.

It is not clear from the archival records whether Teymourtash was seeking new vessels or second-hand vessels. Discussions within the British Government appear to have interpreted it as a request for new vessels, however the record of the discussion held on 1 April 1930 appears to suggest that provision of second-hand vessels was envisaged. In the event no vessels were obtained from Britain.

Note. There are some discrepancies in the anglicised spelling of Persian warship names. Pahlavi is often spelt Pehlevi in contemporary documents, while Shahin is also sometimes spelt as Shahan.
