Proposed transfers of RN Gunboats and Launches to the Canton Government for anti-piracy activities

HMS Nessus (ex-Dom Joao) was one of three river steamers (along with HMS Faulknor, ex-Po On and HMS Onslaught, ex-Shun Lee) that were chartered in Hong Kong for anti-piracy duties in 1925-26. They were small vessels built for commercial work, but were used for duties of a quasi-combatant character. They were fitted with bulletproof armour plating around bridges and gun positions, and were armed with one 3-pdr gun, and four machine guns.

In 1928 the Canton authorities began to show goodwill towards Britain by taking military action against the Guangdong pirates, and the Cantonese authorities commissioned Hong Kong shipbuilders to produce launches specifically for use against pirates on inland waterways, asking Britain to supply the armament and equipment required for the vessels. In January-March 1928 Britain agreed to supply the arms, equipment and ammunition they required provided the Cantonese authorities gave a written undertaking that the purchases would be used solely for anti-piracy work.

The 1928 procurement involved two small river gunboats (Kien Yu (Chien Yu) and Chiang Chien), and two small launches intended for use on inland waterways. The Guangdong Naval Command is reported to have later renamed the vessels in memory of revolutionary martyrs, including Zhongyuan, Jianru, Zhongkai and Zhixin.

This naval procurement was sensitive because it risked breaching the multilateral arms embargo that had been in place since 1919 pending the establishment of a government whose authority was recognised throughout China. The British Minister to China therefore submitted the proposed transaction to the diplomatic corps in Peking for approval. The ministers generally agreed as the weapons and equipment would be restricted to anti-piracy use. The Japanese Minister initially objected, especially to the largest gunboat, which he thought Canton would very likely use as a naval auxiliary in hostilities, however Japan withdrew its objections following British representations.

In January 1929, the British Minister in China submitted a second proposal for the production of anti-piracy craft to the diplomatic corps in Peking, this time for two small launches. It is not known if these two launches were built, however two more river gunboats (Kin Yu and Chip Shun) were built for China in Hong Kong in 1929, and two more (Chai We and Chang Chiang) in 1931-32.

In addition to the four vessels then being built, in March 1928 Li Jishen, Chairman of the Guangdong Provincial Government, also expressed interest in purchasing the British river gunboat HMS Robin. Robin was an 85-ton vessel built in 1897, and at the time was based in Hong Kong as part of the Royal Navy China Squadron. The British declined the Chinese request in view of the restrictions of the Washington Naval Treaty on disposing of a vessel of war in such a manner that it may become a vessel of war in the navy of a foreign power, and she was instead sold locally in Hong Kong in September 1928.

On 8 March 1928, the Hong Kong government suggested that as an alternative to Robin, the three armed launches Faulknor, Onslaught and Nessus be transferred to the Canton authorities for use against piracy, however this did not happen at that time. But in 1929 the three launches were withdrawn from service as the Canton Government had taken over anti-piracy duties and conditions had improved. It was once again proposed to sell Nessus to the Canton Government. In this case, the Admiralty considered that it would be pedantic to regard her modifications as bringing her within the category of a vessel of war in relation to the restrictions on selling second-hand warships under the Washington Naval Treaty. Nessus was sold that year, but it is not yet established who she was sold to.

In 1930 the Admiralty considered a request by Whampoa Sam Co to re-sell the ex-British gunboat Robin to a Canton merchant for use as a houseboat. In view of the fact that by then the vessel had had her engines and armament removed, was over 30 years old, was only fit to be scrapped when sold in 1928, and the prospective owner was prepared to execute a bond that she would not be used as a mobile craft, it was considered most improbable that any power would consider the sale as an infringement of the Washington Treaty.
